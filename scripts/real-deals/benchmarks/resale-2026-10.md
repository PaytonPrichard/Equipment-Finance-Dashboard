# Equipment resale value and useful life benchmarks, October 2026

Tests the equipment assumptions that public ABS data could not test (see `abs-2026-10.md`, section 3):

1. The used-equipment haircut: used equipment valued at 85% of stated cost. New equipment at 100%.
2. The term-to-useful-life limit: a term above 80% of useful life is weak.
3. The useful-life ranges by equipment type in `src/modules/equipment-finance/constants.ts`.

Joel's expectations were committed before any data (`resale-expectations.json`, commit 787b870). He declined to give numbers. His one directional view: 0% is not enough for new equipment.

Researched 2026-10-09. Every figure is from a source loaded that day. The full extracts, with page, table and line citations, are in `resale-sources/`. Figures marked **derived** are arithmetic on cited figures. The loan schedules in section 3 are produced by `resale-analysis.js`.

## What the haircut means

For used equipment, the stated cost is the price the borrower paid on the used market. So the 15% cannot be depreciation. It is the gap between the price paid (retail) and what a lender recovers by selling the collateral (liquidation). The OCC asks for the same basis: "The appraisals should provide a NOLV as the standard for collateral valuation, not cost or retail value" (Comptroller's Handbook, Asset-Based Lending, v1.1, p. 21).

New equipment faces the same gap, plus the drop when it leaves the dealer. So the test for both is: **price paid vs liquidation value.**

## 1. Used equipment: price paid vs liquidation value

| Source | Equipment | Gap below retail or asking | Market |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sandhills, Jan-Aug 2026 | Tractors 100 HP+ | EVI spread 31-33% | Normal |
| Sandhills, Jan-Aug 2026 | Combines | EVI spread 36-38% | Normal |
| Sandhills, Jun 2024 | Tractors 100 HP+ / combines | 45% / 57% (record highs) | Glut |
| Sandhills, Dec 2021 | Sleeper trucks | Auction 17% below asking (39% in Apr 2020, 18% in Apr 2011) | Tight vs glut |
| Sandhills, Dec 2021 | Dozers | 24% (31% in Apr 2020) | Tight vs glut |
| Sandhills, Dec 2021 | High-HP tractors | 14% (22% in Mar 2015) | Tight vs glut |
| Iron Solutions, 2019-2020 sales | 2 tractor models, closed retail | Retail 15% and 38% above auction. Auction = 87% and 72.5% of retail (**derived**) | Normal |
| SBA SOP 50 10 8 | Used machinery | 50% of net book value, or 80% of an orderly liquidation appraisal | Policy |
| Maas Companies blog (vendor, no data cited) | Heavy equipment | OLV 60-80% of FMV, FLV 40-60% | Rule of thumb |

**Caveats.**
- Sandhills does not state its spread formula. The 2022 and 2024 wording reads as asking ÷ auction − 1. If so, a 32% spread means auction at about 76% of asking (**derived**), a 24% gap.
- Asking prices sit above closed retail prices. So the asking-to-auction spread overstates the gap from what a borrower actually paid.
- Auction is close to forced liquidation. A lender with time for an orderly sale should recover more.
- No 2026 spread is published for construction or trucks.

**Finding.** Across products and cycles, the gap between retail and auction runs about 15% to 40%. It is typically 25-30% in 2026 farm data, after the formula adjustment. 15% appears only in tight-inventory snapshots (2011 and 2021 trucks, 2021 tractors) and for one of the two Iron Solutions models. **15% is the low end, a tight-market number.** No source supports it as a through-the-cycle haircut.

## 2. New equipment: is 0% defensible?

| Source | Equipment | Value after year 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Iowa State A3-29, Table 1a | Tractors 80-149 hp and 150+ hp, 400 hrs/yr | 68% and 67% of new list price (auction, as-is) |
| Iowa State A3-29, Table 1a | Combine, 300 hrs/yr | 69% of list |
| Iowa State A3-29, Table 1b | Planter, drill, sprayer | 65% of list |
| BEA geometric rates (type A, from used-asset prices) | Construction machinery, construction tractors, trucks (trucking), farm tractors | 84.5%, 83.7%, 82.8%, 85.5% (**derived**: 1 − rate) |
| SBA SOP 50 10 8 | New machinery | Valued at no more than 75% of price |
| Loeb Appraisal (ABL Advisor, 2015), illustration only | A machine | Forced liquidation value at one year = 65% of new cost |

**Caveats.**
- Iowa State measures against list price. Buyers pay less than list. Its only discount figure is 12.5%, assumed for one worked example. At that discount, a 67%-of-list tractor is worth 77% of what the buyer paid (**derived**).
- BEA's curve is for a whole cohort, including early retirements. BEA says this makes it fall faster early than a single surviving machine would. It also measures a market value, not a forced sale.
- No public new-to-year-1 figure was found for Class 8 trucks or construction equipment.

**Finding.** No source supports valuing new equipment at 100% of price for collateral. Year-1 value runs from about 65% (auction vs list, farm) to 85% (BEA market value). The SBA's 75% of price falls inside that band. **Joel's directional view, that 0% is not enough, is supported.** The data also suggests new equipment loses at least as much in year 1 as the retail-to-auction gap on used equipment.

## 3. Term to useful life: where the collateral shortfall actually sits

`resale-analysis.js` runs Tranche's own structure, a fully amortizing EFA, against the cited value curves. Gap = loan balance minus collateral value, % of cost, worst year-end. Positive means underwater. Loan rate 8%, an illustrative assumption. Balance paths move little with rate. Selected rows at 15% down:

| Curve | 36 mo | 48 mo | 60 mo | 72 mo | 84 mo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ISU tractor 150+ hp, paid list | -8 [y1] | -1 [y1] | +4 [y1] | +7 [y1] | +9 [y1] |
| ISU tractor 150+ hp, paid 12.5% under list | -18 [y1] | -10 [y1] | -6 [y1] | -3 [y1] | -1 [y1] |
| ISU combine, paid list | -10 [y1] | -3 [y1] | +2 [y1] | +5 [y1] | +7 [y2] |
| BEA construction machinery (10-yr life) | -26 [y1] | -18 [y1] | -14 [y1] | -10 [y2] | -6 [y2] |
| BEA trucks, trucking (10-yr life) | -24 [y1] | -17 [y1] | -12 [y1] | -7 [y2] | -2 [y3] |

The script output also covers 10% and 20% down, and four more curves.

**Findings.**
- **The worst point is year 1 or 2, never near maturity.** In an amortizing loan, the balance falls faster late in the term than the collateral does. The shortfall comes from the drop at purchase and in the first year.
- **Down payment and the year-1 drop drive the risk, not term length.** At auction values against list (ISU), a 60-month loan with 15% down is underwater in year 1. Under BEA's market-value curves, no term up to 72 months is underwater at 15% down.
- At 80% of BEA service life, value is 22-32% of new (**derived**). A fully amortizing loan is close to zero by then. So the 80% limit is a reasonable outer guard. It is not where the shortfall comes from.
- This changes for structures with a balloon or residual (TRAC, FMV leases). There, value at maturity is exactly what matters. Tranche's term-coverage factor does not model residual-based structures differently.

Herc cross-check: Herc's rental fleet sold at 37.0-44.9% of original cost a year in FY2017-2025, at an average age of 79-94 months (Herc decks). BEA's construction machinery curve gives about 31% at 7 years (**derived**). Herc does better than the curve. Its fleet is mostly aerial and material handling. It sells partly through retail channels. And BEA's cohort curve runs low for surviving units. So BEA is a conservative curve for well-maintained rental fleets.

## Decisions for Joel

1. **Used haircut.** 15% sits at the tight-market end of every source. Options:
   - (a) keep it
   - (b) raise it to 25-30%, the typical 2026 retail-to-auction gap
   - (c) set it by equipment type. The data is farm-heavy, and only the 2021 Sandhills figures cover trucks and dozers.
2. **New haircut.** 0% has no support. The direct fix, valuing new at 75-85% of cost, pushes LTV on a standard 15%-down deal to 100-113%. Most market-standard deals would then fail the 100% gate. Captives lend at 10-20% down against invoice (ABS benchmarks). Two ways to read that:
   - (a) The gate is meant to be on cost, as the market does it. Keep LTV on cost for the gate, and add a separate liquidation-value metric that shows the year-1 shortfall.
   - (b) The gate is meant to be on liquidation value. Then the 100% gate and the LTV curve need recalibrating at the same time.
3. **Condition bonus (+12 for new).** The collateral data does not support new equipment holding value better than used in year 1. If new gets a haircut, the bonus double-counts.
4. **Term coverage 80%.** Supported as an outer limit. The shortfall sits in years 1-2 and is driven by down payment. A "collateral vs balance through the term" view is a possible backlog item.
5. **Useful-life ranges.** Construction top end 15 vs BEA 8-10. Energy/Power Generation covers assets lasting 8 to 33 years. Others consistent or conservative.
